The results of this study show that biochars produced by pyrolysis at various temperatures from slaughterhouse waste and modified by steam activation exhibit distinct physicochemical properties that make them potentially suitable for various applications. However, several aspects remain open for improvement, and further research can be conducted.

\vspace{0.3em}

A significant improvement for future research would be to characterize the biochar individually after each treatment step, particularly after activation and the sulfur loading process. In this work, BET analysis was performed for biochars that have been activated using steam and loaded with sulfur. Therefore, it is impossible to distinguish the effects of steam activation on the SSA individually and qualitatively, since sulfur can be deposited in the internal pore structure and block the pore entrances after sulfur loading, causing the measured SSA to be reported lower than the surface area that was actually accessible immediately after activation.

\vspace{0.3em} 

Further optimizations could focus on improving the experimental setup used for both steam activation and sulfur loading. In this work, water was introduced manually using a syringe, which may not generate sufficient pressure to ensure that the injected water reaches the section of the tube where evaporation into steam occurs. As a result, some of the water may not have evaporated before entering the reaction zone. Therefore, a water supply system connected to the tube is recommended for future experiments. This would allow the water to be supplied and ensure that it can evaporate into steam before contacting the biochar. The sulfur-loading device could also be optimized. The use of cotton to hold the biochar in place inside the tube can lead to measurement errors, as the uneven distribution of the cotton over time can result in uneven gas flow, which, in turn, leads to uneven adsorption. 

\vspace{0.3em}

%The sulfur-loading experiment provides a first indication of the practical application potential of the biochar. However, other proposed applications should also be validated experimentally rather than being assessed only from material properties. For example, the suitability of the biochar as a soil amendment could be investigated through small-scale plant-growth experiments comparing crops grown in biochar-amended soil with those grown in untreated soil. Such tests could help determine whether the biochar actually improves plant growth, nutrient retention, or other soil properties. A similar approach should be applied to the other potential applications identified in this study.

\vspace{0.3em} 

For future research, the investigation can be narrowed down to biochar produced at pyrolysis temperatures of 400°C and 500°C from French digestate since both showed promising characteristics. The influence of the activation temperature should also be examined more systematically by applying higher temperatures than those used in 
the present work.



 
 Based on the literature, steam activation at temperatures in
the range of approximately 800--850~$^\circ$C may promote stronger pore
development and could therefore be investigated to determine whether a higher
specific surface area can be achieved.



In addition to BET analysis, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) could be used
to compare the surface morphology of the biochar before and after activation.
This would provide a visual indication of structural changes caused by the
activation process, such as the opening or enlargement of pores. SEM should,
however, be regarded as a complementary method, since the microporous surface
responsible for a large part of the BET surface area cannot be fully evaluated
from SEM images alone.
