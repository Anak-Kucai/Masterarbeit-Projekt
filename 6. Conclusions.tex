\section{Conclusions}
\subsection{Key Findings}
\setresponsible{Surya Salim}
\label{subsec:key-findings}
 
Biochar was produced from French (and also Algerian) slaughterhouse digestate at 400, 500, and 600\,$^{\circ}$C. The French chars were then steam-activated at 700\,$^{\circ}$C and loaded with sulfur from H$_2$S. The French digestate gave the higher yield throughout, from 45.8 down to 42.2\,\% compared to 37.4 down to 33.5\,\% for the Algerian material, whose values reproduce those of the preceding project work and confirm that the pyrolysis procedure is repeatable across the two campaigns (Section~\ref{subsec:results-pyrolysis}). Both feedstocks lose only about four percentage points of yield across 200\,$^{\circ}$C, since the mineral fraction stays in the solid while the volatiles leave. That same mineral fraction governs most of what follows. Ash rises from 22.6\,\% in the digestate to 43.5--50.0\,\% in the chars, which keeps the measured calorific value flat at 14.1--14.2\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ and gives an energy densification factor of 0.86.On an ash-free basis, the same chars reach 25.1 to 28.2\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ and a factor of 1.18--1.33. The organic fraction, therefore, carbonized normally, and the shortfall is due to dilution rather than incomplete pyrolysis (Section~\ref{subsubsec:calorific}). CHNS agrees: ash-free carbon rises from 67.2 to 76.0\,\% with most of the change below 500\,$^{\circ}$C, while H/C falls from 0.481 to 0.163 and then to a common floor of 0.12--0.13 once the chars have been activated (Section~\ref{subsubsec:chns}). Steam activation removed 17.9, 15.9, and 11.1\,\% of the char mass at 0.5\,mL\,g$^{-1}$, with the loss decreasing as the parent pyrolysis temperature rises. It should be read as an upper limit on carbon conversion rather than as a measure of pore formation because the 400\,$^{\circ}$C char lost the most mass without ending up with the largest surface area (Section~\ref{subsec:results-activation}). The BET data are the clearest result of the work. The pristine chars are effectively non-porous at 0.78 and 1.80\,m$^2$\,g$^{-1}$ at 400 and 500\,$^{\circ}$C. After activation and sulfur loading, the series measures 100.97, 146.09, and 88.64\,m$^2$\,g$^{-1}$, with the maximum belonging to the 500\,$^{\circ}$C precursor rather than to the most carbonized char. Every sample took up sulfur, 36 to 64\,mg\,g$^{-1}$ by weighing, and the loaded chars carry 5.2--6.8\,\% S against about 1.5\,\% in the pristine biochars (Sections~\ref{subsec:results-sulfur} and~\ref{subsubsec:chns}).
 
\vspace{0.3em}
 
Read together, these numbers favor steam over the CO$_2$ route of the preceding project work for this feedstock and this purpose. Steam removed more mass at every pyrolysis temperature, most visibly 10--11\,\% against roughly 2\,\% at 600\,$^{\circ}$C, and the steam-activated chars adsorbed two to three times more sulfur than the CO$_2$-activated ones, 36--64 against 12--24\,mg\,g$^{-1}$, though the two digestates were not identical and the CO$_2$ char was never measured by BET (Section~\ref{subsec:h2o-vs-co2}). No single pyrolysis temperature is best for every purpose. The 500\,$^{\circ}$C char is the better precursor for adsorption and therefore for biogas desulfurization, the 400\,$^{\circ}$C char retains the most nitrogen at 2.74\,\% and is the more interesting candidate for soil fertility, and the 600\,$^{\circ}$C char has the lowest H/C ratio and the most stable structure for carbon storage, while the ash content rules all three out as a solid fuel against charcoal at over 27\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ (Section~\ref{subsec:utilization}). The concept is worth pursuing as a specific proposition rather than a general one. At 146\,m$^2$\,g$^{-1}$ the material stays far below the several hundred to over a thousand m$^2$\,g$^{-1}$ of commercial activated carbon, so the case rests not on peak adsorption but on a residue feedstock that normally costs money to dispose of and a performance that is adequate for a low to moderate duty task. Removing H$_2$S from the biogas produced by the same digestion, and returning the sulfur-loaded char to the soil afterwards, is the application these data support most directly (Section~\ref{subsec:assessment}).

The results of this study show that biochars produced by pyrolysis at various temperatures from slaughterhouse waste and modified by steam activation exhibit distinct physicochemical properties that make them potentially suitable for various applications. However, several aspects remain open for improvement, and further work can be conducted.

\vspace{0.3em}

A significant improvement for future research would be to characterize the biochar individually after each treatment step, particularly after activation and the sulfur loading process. In this work, BET analysis was performed for biochars that have been activated using steam and loaded with sulfur. Therefore, it is impossible to distinguish the effects of steam activation on the SSA individually and qualitatively, since sulfur can be deposited in the internal pore structure and block the pore entrances after sulfur loading, causing the measured SSA to be reported lower than the surface area that was actually accessible immediately after activation.

\vspace{0.3em} 

Further optimizations could focus on improving the experimental setup used for both steam activation and sulfur loading. In this work, water was introduced manually using a syringe, which may not generate sufficient pressure to ensure that the injected water reaches the section of the tube where evaporation into steam occurs. As a result, some of the water may not have evaporated before entering the reaction zone. Therefore, a water supply system connected to the tube is recommended for future experiments. This would allow the water to be supplied and ensure that it can evaporate into steam before contacting the biochar. The sulfur-loading device could also be optimized. The use of cotton to hold the biochar in place inside the tube can lead to measurement errors, as the uneven distribution of the cotton over time can result in uneven gas flow, which, in turn, leads to uneven adsorption. 

\vspace{0.3em}

%The sulfur-loading experiment provides a first indication of the practical application potential of the biochar. However, other proposed applications should also be validated experimentally rather than being assessed only from material properties. For example, the suitability of the biochar as a soil amendment could be investigated through small-scale plant-growth experiments comparing crops grown in biochar-amended soil with those grown in untreated soil. Such tests could help determine whether the biochar actually improves plant growth, nutrient retention, or other soil properties. A similar approach should be applied to the other potential applications identified in this study.

\vspace{0.3em} 

For future work, the study can be narrowed to biochar produced from French digestate at pyrolysis temperatures of 400 °C and 500 °C, as both exhibited promising characteristics. To improve the physicochemical properties of the biochar, activation at higher temperatures above 700 °C would be highly recommended, as activated biochar with the highest reactivity has reportedly been activated at a temperature of 800 °C \cite{Sajjadi2019}. Therefore, future work could investigate whether this increased reactivity also leads to greater pore formation and a larger specific surface area (SSA) for the biochar examined in this study.

\vspace{0.3em} 

In addition, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) could be conducted for future work to complementary the result of BET analysis. It observes the surface morphology of biochar before and after activation and provides visual evidence of structural changes caused by the activation process, such as the opening or enlargement of pores.
