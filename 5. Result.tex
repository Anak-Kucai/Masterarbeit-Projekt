\section{Results}



\subsection{Pyrolysis for Biochar Production}
\label{subsec:results-pyrolysis}
The biochar yield was determined gravimetrically for both the French and the
Algerian slaughterhouse digestate at 400, 500 and 600\,$^{\circ}$C. To accommodate
the limited quantity of available material, each digestate portion was divided
across three crucibles and pyrolyzed under identical conditions (heating rate
10\,$^{\circ}$C\,min$^{-1}$, residence time 1\,h, with the crucible water held at
approximately 20\,g in every run to maintain a comparable steam environment). The
three crucibles act as replicates; the mean yields are plotted in
Figure~\ref{fig:pyrolysis-yield}, and the complete per-crucible breakdown is given
in the appendix (Table~\ref{tab:pyrolysis-yield-full}). Yield is expressed as the
ratio of recovered biochar to the as-received digestate charge.

Both feedstocks behave consistently within each run, with the replicate standard
deviation staying below half a percentage point throughout. Across the temperature
range the French digestate returns the higher solid yield, falling from 45.7\,\% at
400\,$^{\circ}$C to 42.2\,\% at 600\,$^{\circ}$C, whereas the Algerian digestate
yields between 37.4\,\% and 33.5\,\% over the same interval. The French char
therefore exceeds the Algerian by roughly eight to nine percentage points at every
temperature.

Because both substrates derive from slaughterhouse waste and were processed under
identical conditions, this difference is attributable to feedstock composition
rather than to the pyrolysis program. The consistently higher French yield
reflects a larger fraction of material retained in the solid phase, which points to
differences in the organic and mineral balance of the two digestates arising from
their respective origins and upstream digestion. This interpretation is taken up
again against the elemental and ash data in Section~\ref{sec:characterisation},
where the mineral content recovered in the char is quantified directly.

Both digestates show a steady decrease in yield as the pyrolysis temperature rises,
amounting to about 3.6 percentage points for the French and 4.0 for the Algerian
material between 400 and 600\,$^{\circ}$C. The downward trend follows the expected
release of volatile matter at higher temperature, as the more thermally labile
fractions are driven off and a more carbonized residue remains
(Section~\ref{subsubsec:Effect of Temperature on Biochar}). The modest size of the
decline is itself informative: in digestate-derived feedstocks, the high mineral
fraction is largely retained in the solid during pyrolysis, so the yield responds
less steeply to temperature than is typical for lignocellulosic biomass
(Section~\ref{subsubsec:digestate-pyrolysis relevance}) \parencite{kujawska-2026, wang-2023}.
The close replicate agreement gives confidence that the differences between
feedstocks and temperatures reflect genuine compositional effects rather than
measurement scatter.

The Algerian runs also connect this work to the earlier Projektarbeit, in which the
same slaughterhouse digestate was pyrolyzed under comparable conditions. The present
yields of 37.4, 34.4 and 33.5\,\% reproduce the earlier values of approximately 37,
34 and 31\,\% \parencite{MartinezHuete2024Projektarbeit}, confirming that the pyrolysis procedure is
reproducible across the two experimental campaigns. Set in the wider context of that
study, the slaughterhouse digestate falls in the same range as the grass-silage
digestate (roughly 34--38\,\%) and well below the bio-waste digestate (72--75\,\%),
whose anomalously high yield was traced to non-degradable residues such as plastics
that do not decompose during pyrolysis \parencite{MartinezHuete2024Projektarbeit}. The French
digestate, at 42--46\,\%, therefore sits at the upper end of the genuinely biogenic
digestates examined across both works, while remaining far below the contaminated
bio-waste material.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{H$_2$O}{H2O}-Activation}
\label{subsec:results-activation}
The steam activation was carried out at 700\,$^{\circ}$C on the French biochar samples. There are in total 3 samples - 400, 500, 600\,$^{\circ}$C. The result that will be reported and discussed here is the mass loss of the biochar before and after activation treatment.

The mass loss was evaluated in two ways. The wet basis compares the mass of the biochar directly before and after activation. The other mass loss also takes into account the condensation of the water from the post activation sample. This moisture-corrected value therefore reflects the mass actually removed from the char and is used here as the primary measure.

\caption{Activation mass loss of the French biochar for both water doses (0.1 and

\label{fig:activation-massloss}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:activation-massloss} shows both measures for the two water doses (0.5\,mL\,g$^{-1}$ and 0.1\,mL\,g$^{-1}$). All four data series show a downtrend as the pyrolysis temperature of the biochar increases. On the moisture-corrected basis, the decrease in mass loss could be seen, from 17.9\,\% at 400\,$^{\circ}$C to 11.1\,\% at 600\,$^{\circ}$C, and the 2\,g scale-up runs follow the same descending order (20.6\,\%, 15.0\,\% and 9.8\,\%; Table~\ref{tab:activation-full}). The uncorrected wet basis further supports this downtrend as shown by the dashed line in Figure~\ref{fig:activation-massloss}.

From this behavior, one can interpret that a higher pyrolysis temperature yields a more carbonized and thermally stable solid in which the volatile fractions have already been burnt off (Section~\ref{subsubsec:Effect of Temperature on Biochar}), so less material remains available for removal during activation. The pattern is actually the counterpart of the pyrolysis line graph in Section~\ref{subsec:results-pyrolysis}. Volatile matter removed during pyrolysis is no longer present during activation, so the more extensively devolatilized high-temperature chars are, the less additional loss there is under activation.
Residual devolatilization also contributes substantially to the mass loss during activation. All three chars were produced below the 700\,$^{\circ}$C activation set point, so reheating releases volatiles that not yet removed through pyrolysis before. The effect is largest for 400\,$^{\circ}$C and smallest for the 600\,$^{\circ}$C char.
