\section{Results}



\subsection{Pyrolysis for Biochar Production}
\label{subsec:results-pyrolysis}
The biochar yield was determined gravimetrically for both the French and the
Algerian slaughterhouse digestate at 400, 500 and 600\,$^{\circ}$C. To accommodate
the limited quantity of available material, each digestate portion was divided
across three crucibles and pyrolyzed under identical conditions (heating rate
10\,$^{\circ}$C\,min$^{-1}$, residence time 1\,h, with the crucible water held at
approximately 20\,g in every run to maintain a comparable steam environment). The
three crucibles act as replicates; the mean yields are plotted in
Figure~\ref{fig:pyrolysis-yield}, and the complete per-crucible breakdown is given
in the appendix (Table~\ref{tab:pyrolysis-yield-full}). Yield is expressed as the
ratio of recovered biochar to the as-received digestate charge.

Both feedstocks behave consistently within each run, with the replicate standard
deviation staying below half a percentage point throughout. Across the temperature
range the French digestate returns the higher solid yield, falling from 45.7\,\% at
400\,$^{\circ}$C to 42.2\,\% at 600\,$^{\circ}$C, whereas the Algerian digestate
yields between 37.4\,\% and 33.5\,\% over the same interval. The French char
therefore exceeds the Algerian by roughly eight to nine percentage points at every
temperature.

Because both substrates derive from slaughterhouse waste and were processed under
identical conditions, this difference is attributable to feedstock composition
rather than to the pyrolysis program. The consistently higher French yield
reflects a larger fraction of material retained in the solid phase, which points to
differences in the organic and mineral balance of the two digestates arising from
their respective origins and upstream digestion. This interpretation is taken up
again against the elemental and ash data in Section~\ref{sec:characterisation},
where the mineral content recovered in the char is quantified directly.

\vspace{0.3em}

Both digestates show a steady decrease in yield as the pyrolysis temperature rises,
amounting to about 3.6 percentage points for the French and 4.0 for the Algerian
material between 400 and 600\,$^{\circ}$C. The downward trend follows the expected
release of volatile matter at higher temperature, as the more thermally labile
fractions are driven off and a more carbonized residue remains
(Section~\ref{subsubsec:Effect of Temperature on Biochar}). The modest size of the
decline is itself informative: in digestate-derived feedstocks, the high mineral
fraction is largely retained in the solid during pyrolysis, so the yield responds
less steeply to temperature than is typical for lignocellulosic biomass
(Section~\ref{subsubsec:digestate-pyrolysis relevance}) \parencite{kujawska-2026, wang-2023}.
The close replicate agreement gives confidence that the differences between
feedstocks and temperatures reflect genuine compositional effects rather than
measurement scatter.

\vspace{0.3em}

The Algerian runs also connect this work to the earlier Projektarbeit, in which the
same slaughterhouse digestate was pyrolyzed under comparable conditions. The present
yields of 37.4, 34.4 and 33.5\,\% reproduce the earlier values of approximately 37,
34 and 31\,\% \parencite{MartinezHuete2024Projektarbeit}, confirming that the pyrolysis procedure is reproducible across the two experimental campaigns. Set in the wider context of that
study, the slaughterhouse digestate falls in the same range as the grass-silage
digestate (roughly 34--38\,\%) and well below the bio-waste digestate (72--75\,\%),
whose anomalously high yield was traced to non-degradable residues such as plastics
that do not decompose during pyrolysis \parencite{MartinezHuete2024Projektarbeit}. The French
digestate, at 42--46\,\%, therefore sits at the upper end of the genuinely biogenic
digestates examined across both works, while remaining far below the contaminated
bio-waste material.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{H$_2$O}{H2O}-Activation}
\label{subsec:results-activation}
The steam activation was carried out at 700\,$^{\circ}$C on the French biochar samples. There are in total 3 samples - 400, 500, 600\,$^{\circ}$C. The result that will be reported and discussed here is the mass loss of the biochar before and after activation treatment.

\vspace{0.3em}

The mass loss was evaluated in two ways. The wet basis compares the mass of the biochar directly before and after activation. The other mass loss also takes into account the condensation of the water from the post activation sample. This moisture-corrected value therefore reflects the mass actually removed from the char and is used here as the primary measure.

Figure~\ref{fig:activation-massloss} shows both measures for the two water doses (0.5\,mL\,g$^{-1}$ and 0.1\,mL\,g$^{-1}$). All four data series show a downtrend as the pyrolysis temperature of the biochar increases. On the moisture-corrected basis, the decrease in mass loss could be seen, from 17.9\,\% at 400\,$^{\circ}$C to 11.1\,\% at 600\,$^{\circ}$C, and the 2\,g scale-up runs follow the same descending order (20.6\,\%, 15.0\,\% and 9.8\,\%; Table~\ref{tab:activation-full}). The uncorrected wet basis further supports this downtrend, as shown by the dashed line in Figure~\ref{fig:activation-massloss}.

\vspace{0.3em}

From this behavior, one can interpret that a higher pyrolysis temperature yields a more carbonized and thermally stable solid in which the volatile fractions have already been burnt off (Section~\ref{subsubsec:Effect of Temperature on Biochar}), so less material remains available for removal during activation. The pattern is actually the counterpart of the pyrolysis line graph in Section~\ref{subsec:results-pyrolysis}. Volatile matter removed during pyrolysis is no longer present during activation, so the more extensively devolatilized high-temperature chars are, the less additional loss there is under activation. Residual devolatilization also contributes substantially to the mass loss during activation. All three chars were produced below the 700\,$^{\circ}$C activation set point, so reheating releases volatiles that have not yet been removed through pyrolysis beforehand. The effect is largest for 400\,$^{\circ}$C and smallest for the 600\,$^{\circ}$C char.

\vspace{0.3em}

As mentioned in section \ref{Physical Activation of Biochar}, steam gasification is the pore-forming reaction, and it depends heavily on a larger steam supply and temperatures above 700\,$^{\circ}$C used here. The 0.5\,mL\,g$^{-1}$ charge may or may not reach an greater carbon conversion despite its smaller total mass loss. Mass loss alone cannot fully depict the two contributions, and the point is left to the BET Results. The 0.5\,mL\,g$^{-1}$ dose was retained as the standard for its more reliable wetting of the char and lower mass loss.

The CO$_2$-activation of the preceding Projektarbeit offers a direct point of comparison, since it also used the same reactor, temperature, and residence time as the steam-activation runs here (700\,$^{\circ}$C, 1\,h). These two slaughterhouse-digestates, though they come from different sources, could provide insight into this discussion. Since no water is sprayed during CO$_2$ activation, the reported before/after loss is assumed to be under low-moisture conditions and can be placed alongside the corrected steam values without adjustment. Based on this, the steam-activated char loses more mass than the CO$_2$-activated char at every pyrolysis temperature (Figure~\ref{fig:co2-vs-steam}; Table~\ref{tab:co2-activation-projektarbeit}). The widest gap could be seen at 600\,$^{\circ}$C, where steam removes about 10--11\,\% compared to roughly 2\,\% for CO$_2$. 
Steam tends toward a more extensive distribution of micropores and mesopores and adds more oxygen-containing surface groups, whereas CO$_2$ works mainly within the micropore range, as mentioned in \ref{Physical Activation of Biochar}.

The gross calorific value of the slaughterhouse digestate from France was 16.41\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$. After pyrolysis, these value are lower for all three biochars at about 4.1--14.2\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:calorific-value}; Table~\ref{tab:calorific-full}). The calorific values of the biochars are visibly lower than that of the raw digestate itself. There are also minimal differences between pyrolysis temperatures, which might suggest that temperature has little effect on calorific value — but the ash trend argues otherwise. Bomb calorimetry reports the heat released per gram of whole sample, which means ash is also included. Ash is an inert mineral matter that adds mass without contributing anything to combustion. Thus, dividing the measured value by the combustible fraction $(1 - \text{ash content})$ removes this ballast. Assuming that ash releases no heat, the ash-free calorific value climbs with pyrolysis temperature, from 21.2\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ for the digestate to 25.1, 27.9, and 28.2\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ at 400, 500, and 600\,$^{\circ}$C respectively. The organic fraction of the biochar becomes more energy-dense as temperature increases, consistent with the progressive loss of oxygen-rich volatiles and the enrichment in fixed carbon that accompany carbonization \parencite{pecchi-2019, hung-2017}.

The high ash content additionally determines where these biochars are applicable. Half the mass of the 600\,$^{\circ}$C and 500\,$^{\circ}$C char is mineral residue, which makes it a poor option for solid fuel. On the other hand, taking the soil-amendment route seems like a better option since the same fraction could carry the mineral nutrients concentrated from the digestate or the biochar and return them to the soil with the carbon. In this context, the ash works in the material's favor; its effect on adsorption is a separate question, addressed along with the BET surface-area results.

\vspace{0.3em}

\textcite{hung-2017} reports about 16.6\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ for a solid digestate, containing roughly 23\,wt\% ash. The 16.41\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ measured here at 22.6\,\% ash closely reproduces that number, which can lay the foundation for the upcoming comparison of the chars. The biochars themselves fall at or below the bottom of the published HHV trend range, shown in table \ref{tab:hhv_digestate_comparison}. Digestate-derived biochars are generally reported to have HHV between 15 and 22\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ after pyrolysis, compared to 14.1--14.2\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ in
this work. Ash-content plays an important role. At roughly 50\,\% for the
600\,$^{\circ}$C char, this places this material at the top of the 15--50\,wt\% band
typical of digestates.

To prove the points a bit further, using the equation~\ref{eq:edf}, the energy densification can be achieved by dividing the biochar calorific value by that of the feedstock. Every biochar has energy densification factor at 0.86, and that means one thing. The pyrolysis lowered the energy density of the solid instead of raising it. Lignocellulosic feedstocks treated over the same 400--600\,$^{\circ}$C window normally reach 1.4--1.8 \parencite{bridgwater-2011, antal-2003}. This differences actually could be expected. For high-ash digestates the factor should stay below 1.4 and can fall to 1 or lower. The reason is because ash dilution outweighs carbon enrichment \parencite{hung-2017, opatokun-2015}, and the present material reaches the lower bound of that prediction. Applying Equation~\ref{eq:energy-yield} with the mass
yields of Section~\ref{subsec:results-pyrolysis} gives energy yields of 39.5,
37.2 and 36.3\,\% at 400, 500 and 600\,$^{\circ}$C. For comparison, lignocellulosic feedstocks yield energy around 50--75\,\%.
However, if compared on an ash-free basis, the same chars read differently. Their 25.1--28.2\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ lies inside the 25--33\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ reported for lignocellulosic
biochar (Table~\ref{tab:hhv_fuels}), and when the densification factor recalculated again, this basis rises to 1.18--1.33. The ash-free char left by pyrolysis is comparable in energy density to wood-derived char, and the shortfall in the measured value comes from ash rather than from poor or incomplete carbonization.
